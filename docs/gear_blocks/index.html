How it fits together

Every rail is a lead screw in its own channel. Its nut rides the channel walls, and a short shaft stands on top of the nut. Turn a knob and that nut, with whatever sits on its shaft, slides along the rail.

  • Mechanisms drop over the shafts. Each has a capped hub that runs on the shaft with a 0.2 mm clearance. The cap rests on the shaft tip and the hub floats just above the collar, so it spins freely. There's no separate stop cap to lose.
  • Meshing across rails. Neighbouring shafts are one carrier spacing apart. Two gears mesh when their centres are m (z₁ + z₂) / 2 + backlash apart, so the builder slides the second nut along until that distance works out on the diagonal. Gears too small to span the spacing can't reach the next rail. The builder says so, and also flags anything that would hit a neighbour's shaft, gear or knob.
  • Geneva drives. A driver on one rail indexes a wheel on another, at the driver's centre distance. Turn on Gear teeth and the driver's plate becomes a spur gear (same module as the rest), so a gear on a third rail can drive it.
  • Multi-start screws. With 4 starts, the nut moves 4 pitches per turn, so 100 mm of travel takes about 14 turns instead of 57. More starts make the screw easier to back-drive: a hard push on a mechanism can turn the knob.
  • Printing. Each STL comes out in assembly position. Print gears and Geneva drivers flat side down. A Geneva wheel sits partway up its hub (to clear its driver's plate), so it needs a little support under the wheel. The tooth profile is the same involute bmech's spur gear generator makes in Blender.
  • Small parts. The smallest loose pieces are gone: no stop cap, and the endstop is as wide as the knob. Nuts, knobs and small gears can still fit a small-parts test cylinder, so this is for ages 3 and up.
